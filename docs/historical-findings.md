# Historical Evidence for Modern Analysis

and validation. A reported correction, distance or parallax claim is evidence of
historical practice; it is not an established modern result.

## Stellar Observations and Annual Aberration

The Paris and Greenwich observing books are candidates for an independent
determination of annual stellar aberration. Their dated readings, corrections and
reference-star dependencies need separate interpretation. Establish whether annual
aberration was already applied or entered through calibration before selecting an
analysis subset. The availability of a book does not establish that its reductions
permit an independent determination.

Published mean stellar positions do not supply an individual seasonal observing
sequence. Planetary positions and mean stellar catalogs retain their own uses and
reduction dependencies. The [research plan](research-plan.md) places the study
alongside the other planned analyses; the [ingestion queue](data-sources.md#planned-ingestions)
lists the source acquisitions it may draw on.

## Mars parallax and stellar refraction experiments, 1587

*Opera Omnia* XI pp.181–187 records February 23 and March 10 observing protocols
for comparing Mars at different altitudes and as it approached setting.
Use the recorded Mars–star separations, declinations, horizontal coordinates,
clock calibrations and stellar comparison measurements to reconstruct those
protocols. The March 10 account explicitly calls for refraction corrections
when comparing the evening and setting observations. Compare historical and
modern corrections separately after ingestion.

The altitude and latitude parallax corrections of 3′55″ and 3′36″ on p.185 are
historical calculation quantities, not established detections. The stellar
refraction discussion on p.186 leaves the units of `1½`, `1 40` and `1 30`
unassigned. Resolve their interpretation before applying them to Mars.

Source: [Dreyer, Opera Omnia XI](https://books.arsastronomica.com/Tycho_Brahe_Thesaurus_Observationum_Tomus_Secundus.pdf).

## Mars corrections and model comparisons, 1588–1589

*Opera Omnia* XI pp.275–276 and 336 gives separate apparent and corrected

The bare correction figures on p.276 leave their units unresolved.

The April 1589 table on p.334 places Tychonic reductions beside Alphonsine and

## Spica refraction table from the Mercury observations, 1586

*Opera Omnia* XI p.84 gives declination and right-ascension refraction components
for Spica at altitudes of 1°–15°. It adopts Spica's true declination as 8°57½′
south and its right-ascension difference from the heart of Leo as 49°19½′,
using the October–November Mercury observations. The catalog retains both
adopted inputs and all fifteen table rows as supporting calibration for
Spica-based Mars reduction studies; historical application to Mars remains
unestablished. Preserve the two equatorial components; they are
not a universal vertical-refraction table or direct Mars measurements. Modern
comparisons need the same component geometry and explicit atmospheric assumptions.
Related component estimates occur on XI p.134 for the Sun near the equinox
and p.173 for Jupiter. Retain their adopted reference positions and stated
refraction estimates together. The solar account concerns declination;
Jupiter's account distinguishes distance and declination and makes a seasonal
claim. Preserve those geometries and historical assumptions in comparisons.
XI pp.204–206 also give Mercury refraction estimates and separate altitude,
distance and declination components, alongside parallax reductions. Stellar
calibration tables on pp.222–223 compare refraction estimates with solar
refraction. Retain the stated geometry, reference values and correction order
with these tables; keep refraction and parallax terms distinct.
The Venus reduction on XI p.277 also compares a Copernican distance with
parallax and refraction terms. Solar reductions on pp.312–313 preserve
alternative correction chains. Retain their stated units, adopted values,
correction order and alternative results as separate historical variants.
The solar sequences continue on pp.313–316, with a further refraction
investigation on pp.324–325. Mercury reductions on p.339 distinguish
refraction and parallax and retain differing printed inputs. The Sagittarius
table on p.362 has an explicit column for added refraction; later entries on
pp.401–402 compare its readings with adopted stellar positions. Keep the
observed readings, added corrections and adopted results separate. These
passages do not establish that the same corrections were applied to Mars.
See [Dreyer, Opera Omnia XI](https://books.arsastronomica.com/Tycho_Brahe_Thesaurus_Observationum_Tomus_Secundus.pdf).

## Mixed reductions and Mars-qualified stellar positions

*Opera Omnia* XI p.273 pairs reductions for Mars and Jupiter on 15 October

Keep those companions and their calculation epochs distinct from measured
separations. On p.407, the note calling a position “Best” after comparison with
Mars observations attaches to the eighteenth Aquarius star's right ascension
from the eighth Aquarius star. Preserve that stellar calibration evidence;
the note does not turn subsequent catalog stars into Mars readings.

## Mars commentary and manuscript calculations

Dreyer's preface to *Opera Omnia* X, pp.XXI and XXV, places the Mars commentary
from the 1585 observation book in Volume V of the *Works*. Locate that edition
for the model comparisons. The preface identifies the commentary in codex L,
leaves 175v–185v, and calculations headed *Pro Apogæo ♂* on leaves 87v–90r.
On pp.XIX–XX it also describes Kepler's explanation of a Mars calculation
under Tycho's hypotheses, inserted in codex I between leaves 172 and 173, and
a Mars-latitude table in codex K, leaves 341–344. These are leads to historical
calculations, not additional measured positions. Establish their printed
locations and relationships to the logs before using them.

Source: [Dreyer, Opera Omnia X](https://books.arsastronomica.com/Tycho_Brahe_Thesaurus_Observationum_Tomus_Primus.pdf).

## Venus and Jupiter comparisons, 1585

The February 28 Venus altitudes on *Opera Omnia* X p.393 have a detached note
on p.394: codex V labels the altitude column as Mars, while the edition says
the target should be Venus. The affected altitudes are represented as editorial
variants under Venus, with the manuscript's Mars label retained as context.
Retain the remaining Venus and Jupiter readings under their own targets.

The March 9 observations and reductions on X pp.394–395 compare Venus and
Jupiter with stellar references and historical tables. They provide a useful
extension of the planetary-model comparisons beyond Mars. Preserve both the
heading naming Alphonsine and Copernican calculations and the table column
labeled Prutenic; those labels do not establish identical parameter sets.
See the [published volume](https://books.arsastronomica.com/Tycho_Brahe_Thesaurus_Observationum_Tomus_Primus.pdf).

The April 26 Venus reductions on X p.399 compare right ascensions obtained
from Regulus and the lower head of Gemini, adopting 91°6′ with a preference
for the comparison less affected by refraction. Retain the separate inputs,
adopted result and proposed addition of about 6′ for parallax near epicycle
perigee. The proposal does not establish an applied correction or a measured
parallax. Its scope is separate from the nearby Mars observations.

The May 6–7 Venus material on X pp.400–401 includes an apogee/eccentricity
investigation, precession variants and comparisons with the Prutenic and
Alphonsine tables. Consider the complete Venus calculations with their stated
parameter choices. The May 7 reduction uses Mars as a reference: its quoted
Mars coordinates and the shared separation belong to the catalog's Mars subset,
with their calculation roles retained. The following Venus parallax terms
are 4½′ in altitude, 2′40″ in longitude and 3′40″ in latitude at a stated
altitude of 20°. Preserve their correction scope and application state;
they are historical Venus reductions, not measured Mars parallax.

The September Venus investigation on X pp.406–407 compares morning and evening
elongations, an apogee shared with the Sun and alternative eccentricity
equations. Retain the observed reductions, the source's own calculation,
Prutenic and Alphonsine columns, and the approximately 13′ discrepancy with
their distinct roles. “Parallax of the orb” in this calculation is a model
equation; it must not become a diurnal parallax measurement.

The Saturn opposition investigations on X pp.407–409 compare equatorial and
sextant distances, several successive reductions and predictions from
Copernican and Alphonsine calculations. Preserve opposition to the simple or
mean Sun as stated, the unequal-motion qualification and the alternative
opposition times and positions. The successive versions must remain distinct.

The Mercury reductions on X pp.412 and 415 provide further historical
correction scenarios: refraction of 9′ or 10′, assumed parallax of 4′, and
separate longitude and latitude adjustments. Consider the observations, altitude
conditions, adopted inputs and resulting coordinates together. These assumed
Mercury parallax terms do not measure Mars's distance. See the
[published volume](https://books.arsastronomica.com/Tycho_Brahe_Thesaurus_Observationum_Tomus_Primus.pdf).

The addenda on X p.428 revisit Aquila/Antinous and Hydra/Crater identifications
and a stellar distance differing between manuscript witnesses. Consider their
original labels, editorial alternatives and computed comparisons together;
an editorial identification must not silently replace a recorded endpoint.

The separate 1585 fixed-star observations on X pp.416–427 contain armillae
comparisons and meridian-altitude work that may support later planetary
reductions. Check explicit links and adopted stellar parameters before using
them for Mars; sharing a date or reference star does not establish that the
same calibration was applied. The section includes instrument alignment from
western observations (p.419), a recorded-versus-expected transit clock check
(p.421), armillae rectification (p.422), and manuscript variants and polar-star
work (pp.423–427). No explicit application to Mars has been established.
Consider the stellar readings for a separate calibration and instrument study.

## Stellar right ascensions from Venus

The investigation on *Opera Omnia* X pp.204–230 derives stellar right ascensions
from Venus observations, including meridian altitudes and comparisons with the
Sun and fixed stars. Keep hypothesis and adopted elevation as separate choices:
the section compares Copernican and Ptolemaic calculations at elevations of
34°5′30″ and 34°9′50″. Retain assumed distances and the refraction and parallax
components. Check explicit links before using these
results as Mars's stellar inputs. Retain local historical star names and their

## Saturn and lunar comparisons, 1570

The March 5 Saturn series on *Opera Omnia* X p.28 gives four stellar distances,
an observing interval after 10½ hours and a later finishing clock. Its detached
notes preserve a 20/50-minute manuscript variant and a restored descriptive word.
Consider the readings, timing scope and witness alternatives together; they do not
extend the preceding Mars series or supply a correction to it. See the
[published volume](https://books.arsastronomica.com/Tycho_Brahe_Thesaurus_Observationum_Tomus_Primus.pdf).

The March 18 lunar account on the same page contrasts a visual Moon–Regulus
spacing expressed in lunar diameters with an instrumental two-degree value.
Retain the original units, the source's criticism of the visual estimate, its
own clock and the qualitative longitude-parallax claim separately. The nearby
Mars account supplies visual geometry and an observing limitation, not these
lunar distances or a Mars parallax measurement.

The March 31 Saturn section on X p.30 preserves three original distances and
three later qualified counterparts, the last explicitly doubtful. Consider both
states, its separate observing clock and the witness notes together. The first
two replacements increase their distances by 5′, whereas the third decreases
its distance by 5′. These printed revisions do not establish a uniform
correction to the neighbouring Mars observations. The following examination on
p.31 gives further Saturn reductions and adopted positions to retain as
additional calculation states.

## Comet parallax and instrument corrections

The closing discussion of the 1577–1578 comet in *Opera Omnia* XIII proposes
parallax tests using the apparent path, fixed-star distances at different hours,
and comparisons with Hájek and Cornelius Gemma. It also discusses clock-wheel
phase and the limitations of methods that assume a stationary comet. Retain the
underlying measurements and methodological claims separately; the argument is
not itself a measured parallax.

The following October 1580 comet account describes a tail directed toward Mars
and explicitly leaves instrumental parallax uncorrected in sextant readings.
Preserve the comet target, qualitative Mars reference and instrumental
correction state without treating them as Mars positions or celestial parallax.
See the [published volume](https://books.arsastronomica.com/Tycho_Brahe_Thesaurus_Observationum_Tomus_Quartus.pdf).

## Fabricius site parameters and historical records sent by Kepler

The closing note to Fabricius's report gives Resterhafe in East Frisia as the
site of all its observations, with a pole elevation of 53°38′ and a probable
historical longitude of 29°30′. These supporting parameters are represented
with the report-wide site scope and unresolved longitude reference meridian.

On XIII p.246, Kepler's submission sent in August 1600 discusses earlier Mars
conjunctions while questioning whether their reported positions came from
observations or tables. Preserve the quoted positions, proposed alternatives
and attribution uncertainty. The same section points to Mars material in the
1584 *Cosmographia* of Apian and Gemma Frisius and reports Venus–Mars and
Jupiter–Mars occultations in 1590–1591. Those events are represented as
historical reports with separate time and timing-boundary qualifications;
the cited external observation remains queued for source review.
The same page cites Johannes Rothmann's 1596 Erfurt work for planetary places
stated to derive from observations with Hessian instruments, and Johannes
Engel's 1510 ephemeris for comments on earlier planetary tables. Locate the
editions and specific records before using their numerical material. See the
[published volume](https://books.arsastronomica.com/Tycho_Brahe_Thesaurus_Observationum_Tomus_Quartus.pdf).

On XIII p.248, the same submission records Jupiter's stellar alignments on
29 September 1595 and a solar eclipse on 3 October. Consider the solar altitudes,
projected-image procedure, explicitly unreliable wooden half-foot quadrant,
alternative inclination readings and Sun/Moon semidiameter comparison together.
The clouds that ended the observation and the instrument warning qualify the
measurements; neither supplies a numerical uncertainty. The Mars sign in the
eclipse paragraph denotes the weekday.

Kepler's pole-star reductions on XIII p.249 are supporting calibration
candidates: distinguish the computed least altitude, other altitude and pole
distance values, the resulting pole elevation and the less accurate
alternatives. The dated-day list does not repeat a month or observing site;
do not assign these parameters to every Mars record. The lunar-diameter and
stellar-baseline comparisons on p.250 are also relevant to interpreting visual
separations, provided their dates, endpoints and uncalibrated angular scale
remain explicit.

## Longomontanus: Astronomia Danica

The [published edition](https://arsastronomica.com/works/longomontanus-astronomia-danica/)
and [PDF](https://books.arsastronomica.com/Longomontanus_Astronomia_Danica.pdf) reproduce
the revised **1640 printing**. The work first appeared in 1622; model parameters and
wording must remain bound to the edition actually used. The [original scan](https://archive.org/details/astronomiadanica00long)
provides the witness for checking figures and diagrams.

Book II of the Theorics is particularly important for model comparison. Its Saturn
chapters set out reformed Ptolemaic, Copernican and Tychonic constructions for the
superior planets. Chapters VIII–X then develop Mars’s first and annual inequalities
and periodic motions; chapters XI–XIV supply tables and computation methods.
Longomontanus’s preference for daily Earth rotation in the general discussion also
requires a distinct model specification rather than an automatic alias for Tycho.

| Material | Representation | Research use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Threefold world ordering and superior-planet geometry | Model assumptions, diagrams and attributed geometric constructions | Compare predictions and identify equivalent descriptions while retaining the author’s modifications. |
| Mars first inequality and fourteen opposition positions, 1580–1610 | Source parameters, opposition definitions, reduced positions and tabular comparisons | Reconstruct Mars geometry; trace overlap with Tycho’s diaries before counting independent observations. |
| Annual inequality, orbital scale and worked triangles | Model equations, distance-scale assumptions and calculation examples | Test the specific circular construction and its treatment of the Mars anomaly. |
| Mars epochs and mean-motion tables | Edition-bound parameters, time arguments and numerical tables | Produce predictions using historical parameters before any refitting. |
| Equation, center-distance and latitude tables | Lookup arguments, values, units and interpolation rules | Reproduce the numerical workflow and coordinate reductions. |
| Solar theory, parallax and refraction | Supporting parameters and correction methods | Reconstruct reduction assumptions without treating computed corrections as parallax detections. |

The worked examples include a 1594 Uraniborg position and a 1613 comparison with a
Gemini star. The latter combines reported star offsets with an inferred conjunction;
it is not an additional raw Tycho observation. These examples, the opposition
table and the theory’s parameter sources require separate records.

Longomontanus is a distinct source work. Its quantities retain their own attribution
and do not alter the four-volume observation catalog or its coverage counts.

## Comparative Evidence in the Supplementary Books

The seven selected editions have been ingested within their stated limits.
These leads concern research applications, documentary relationships and unresolved
interpretations. See the [coverage statement](historical-books-ingestion.md) and
[published editions, PDFs and original witnesses](sources.md#additional-observations-ephemerides-and-theories).
Further editions and unselected parts remain in the [ingestion queue](data-sources.md#planned-ingestions).

### Snellius: Observationes Hassiacae, 1618

Begin with *Observations of the Planets*, including the explicitly headed
*Observations of Mars and Jupiter by the Sextant*. The September 13 and 29, 1597
examples contain Mars–Jupiter and Mars–star separations, repeated readings and
observing times. Preserve the Hessian observers' attributions, reference-star
coordinates, instruments and clock conventions. Unresolved printed fractions
remain unresolved numerical expressions.

The *Gleaning from the Bohemian Observations of Tycho Brahe* supplies an additional
publication witness, including Mars meridian altitudes at the beginning of 1600
and observing-site parameters. The 1599 heading explicitly specifies Gregorian
dates; calendar scope must be established for each subsequent section. Compare
these records with the diaries before merging identities or counting new evidence.
The Regiomontanus, Peuerbach and Walther collections provide a later extension
to earlier observations, with their original observer and source lineage retained.

Brahe's March 1570 comparison of the Aldebaran–northern-Taurus-horn baseline with
Walther's 1486 measurement points specifically to Snellius, fol.32 verso
(*Opera Omnia* X, p.27). Consider the two publications as witnesses to the historical
calibration, preserving the distinction between Walther's inputs, the calculated
distances and Tycho's own comparison reading.

### Riccioli: Astronomia Reformata, 1665

Book VII, *Observations of the Planet Mars, and the Hypothesis of Their Motions*,
is a priority for additional Mars evidence and comparison witnesses. It collects
ancient records, Regiomontanus, Walther, Copernicus, Tycho, Bürgi, Longomontanus,
Kepler, Fabricius, Sascerides and later observers. The contents locate the Tycho
section at p.319 and the Bürgi–Longomontanus–Kepler–Fabricius–Sascerides section
at p.321. The Tycho heading specifies old-style dates and hours after midday,
with Uraniborg as the default site unless otherwise stated.

Retain reported observations, later alternative reductions and table comparisons
as linked but distinct records. Trace overlaps with both the diaries and Snellius
by observer, date, location and publication lineage, without selecting identities
through numerical agreement. Preserve quoted historical dates alongside later
calendar conversions and disputed datings.

The Mars hypothesis supplies mean motions, apogee and node motions, an epoch on
the Bologna meridian and numerical parameters. The Mars longitude, latitude and
orbit-to-ecliptic computation rules are additional model inputs. Consider these
separately from observations. The astronomical refraction/parallax treatment,
solar refraction tables and comparisons with other observers also belong to the
[correction comparison program](correction-comparisons.md). Historical correction
values and inferred distances are not modern reference values or new detections.

### Ursus: Fundamentum Astronomicum, 1588

Chapter V, *On the Observation of the Motions of the Planets*, its astronomical
theses and the *Explanation of the Diagram of the Hypotheses* define a separate
model-comparison source. The text explicitly combines a stationary terrestrial
location with daily terrestrial rotation. Record that formulation, its diagram,
motions and scale assumptions rather than treating “Ursine” as an interchangeable
label for every Tychonic construction. Establish whether the available parameters
fully specify numerical Mars predictions before implementing a model.

Chapters II–IV supply sine construction, spherical and plane triangle solutions,
and stellar coordinate reductions. These support reconstruction of historical
calculation methods. Worked geometry and theoretical periods retain their roles;
they do not become a dated Mars observation series.

### Origanus: Ephemerides Brandenburgicae, 1609

The *Introductio* provides ephemeris interpretation, worked examples and planetary
geometry. Chapter VI addresses the equation of time and reduction to other
terrestrial meridians; its tables and the surrounding clock-conversion procedures
are relevant to historical prediction times. Preserve the stated meridian, day
boundary, time scale, correction direction and calculation basis.

The title's double Tychonic/Copernican calculation explicitly concerns the
luminaries. Determine the basis of each planetary quantity rather than assuming
two independent Mars predictions. The advertised 1595–1655 span describes the
ephemeris collection; establish which daily tables are present in the chosen
witness before queuing a full numerical series. Predictions and computed aspects,

## Earlier Mars editions and manuscript witnesses

Dreyer identifies Lalande's French publication of the 1593 Mars observations in
*Mémoires de l'Académie* (1757), p.411 ff., as drawing on Bartholin's copy rather
than De l'Isle's. Consider the published pages as an additional comparison witness;
their readings have not yet been recovered or collated. Dreyer's note also warns
that Lalande described readings through both sight vanes as readings by two
instruments. See the [Volume X preface, p.IX and note 3](https://arsastronomica.com/works/tycho-brahe-thesaurus-observationum-tomus-primus/).

calculations in the Vienna books absent from the Copenhagen books (pp.XI–XII).
Retain those source relationships before comparing versions. Calculations,
observational entries, later copies and an editor's reliability judgements need
separate records; none supplies a measured transcription error rate.
