# Edition findings

These findings document discrepancies in the [published editions](sources.md) and their printed-source witnesses. Corrections remain separate from the recorded readings. The [review policy](edition-issues.md) distinguishes edition problems, printed-source variants and pipeline defects.

Browse findings for [Origanus](#origanus), the [Progymnasmata](#progymnasmata)
and the [Thesaurus Observationum](#thesaurus-observationum). Each entry retains
its source scope and interpretation limits; inclusion here does not imply a
confirmed translation error or an automatically applied correction.

## Origanus

### Origanus: proportional-table coverage

The published rendering of the folded proportional-motion table, inserted
after printed p.140, supplies four rows of the upper triangle, the lower-left
groups 1–8 and the final row across all thirty groups. These are the panels
available for numerical ingestion; the other interiors remain unrepresented.
This is a coverage limitation, not evidence that the supplied numerals are
inaccurate. Structural blanks, queried entries and the overlap between panel
renderings remain distinct. No missing cell is reconstructed from a numerical
pattern. See the [published source directory](sources.md).

### Origanus: differences within worked predictions

Part II, chapter III contains several statements that remain distinct in the
catalog. The hourly-motion example ends with 13 June, although the preceding
example uses 13 August; the edition's note identifies the printed month as
June. In the September example, the first lunar table entry carries Libra,
while its header and the later working use Virgo. The Vienna example proposes
15 April but closes by referring to the “5th day.” These are documented
differences within the supplied edition. No numeral, zodiac sign or date is
silently replaced, and the catalog does not assign a settled cause to every
difference. See the [published source directory](sources.md).

### Origanus: two lunar-latitude calculation results

Part II, chapter IV gives a direct lunar-latitude calculation followed by a
prosthaphaeresis calculation of the same example. The first prints the sine
485475 and latitude 2°46′57″; the second prints 486475 while calling its result
“as before.” Both numerical occurrences remain unchanged. This is a difference
within the supplied edition; its cause has not been established, and no
transcription correction or reconstructed replacement has been adopted.
See the [published source directory](sources.md).

### Origanus: ascensional-radix headers

Part II, chapter VI, printed p.199 labels the first two ascensional-radix
columns ♈ and ♉. The edition's notes identify the corresponding rows as ♎
and ♏. The catalog preserves both printed headers and their qualifications;
it does not replace the numerical entries or infer their argument signs from
the size of the values. See the [published source directory](sources.md).

## Progymnasmata

### Progymnasmata: minute panel described as hours

In the *In Horis et Minutis* table, printed p.14, the English description,
right-panel caption and index label describe 31–60 as hours. The
[printed heading](https://www.e-rara.ch/i3f/v20/84309/350,360,1600,300/full/0/default.jpg)
places that panel in the minute band with angular M/S columns. The left panel
explicitly supports both H/G-M-S and Mi/M-S-T readings. The proposed correction
is to label the right panel minutes 31–60, including the reference to minute45.
This is a confirmed descriptive-unit issue; no numerical transcription change
is indicated. The catalog records the right-hand arguments in minutes and
retains both uses of the left-hand figures.

### Progymnasmata: two interval and attribution clarifications

The edition's note 685 identifies two wording limitations in the discussion of
Gemma's observations. On printed p.560, “the difference between the polar and
the polar distance” obscures two distinct intervals: the New Star–Pole Star
separation and the Pole Star's distance from the pole. On p.561, “by his paternal
contrast” is left in the text where the note explains the sense as “from his
father's observation.” These are documented translation qualifications, not
numerical transcription errors. The proposed clarifications remain separate
from the edition's wording. See the [published source directory](sources.md).

### Progymnasmata: suspected Hainzel identification discrepancy

On printed p.566, the bracketed identification of the Augsburg consul names
Johann Baptist Hainzel when recalling the 7 November sighting. The earlier
account quotes that testimony in Paul Hainzel's letter, signed with his name
on p.538. This is an internal attribution discrepancy requiring clarification;
neither name is silently substituted, and no date or numerical reading changes.
See the [published source directory](sources.md).

### Progymnasmata: unit question in a recalled parallax bound

On printed pp.701–702, the English account twice recalls a limit as “three
seconds”; the earlier Kassel discussion gives a bound in minutes. The relation
between these statements and the printed unit wording remains unresolved. This
is a suspected unit ambiguity requiring a targeted source check. Retain the
edition's wording and qualify its interpretation; no correction is adopted.
See the [published source directory](sources.md).

## Thesaurus Observationum

### Suspected incomplete glyph note: XI p.232

The editorial note explaining the uncertain sign after “lower head” ends
mid-comparison, after “like”, with an opening quotation mark. Its explanation
appears truncated. The tentative Gemini identification remains qualified;
no missing wording is supplied and no numerical reading is changed.
See the [published sources](sources.md), XI p.232.

### Unresolved solar zodiac signs: XII p.413

The solar longitudes 2°14⅓′ and 2°6¼′ retain unresolved signs in the edition.
The printed glyphs suggest Scorpio, recorded as a candidate identification for
further witness review. Both numerical angles remain unchanged, with no accepted
zodiac origin or corrected coordinate. The solar right ascension is not used to
supply a missing sign. See the [published edition and original scan](sources.md),
XII p.413.

### Suspected lunar-altitude header ambiguity: XII p.411

The Moon–Mars table is continuous in the print, with a local “upper limb”
annotation for 58°20′. The edition splits the table and repeats a “lower horn”
header above the later 57°35′ altitude. That added header may overstate the
later reading's limb association. The catalog preserves 57°35′ as a lunar
altitude with an unresolved limb, alongside the explicitly labeled lower-horn
57°45′ and upper-limb 58°20′ readings. This is a suspected layout ambiguity;
neither a numerical correction nor a settled limb identification is adopted.
See the [published edition and original scan](sources.md), XII p.411.

### Daily-motion wording: XII p.298

The December 19 comparison says “reduced by the daily motion for 34 M.” The
Latin attaches 34 M. to the daily motion; parallel passages use the same
terminology for angular daily rates. The catalog therefore records 34′ per
source day, separately from the calculation epochs. The English “for” is
documented as a suspected quantity ambiguity because it may suggest a
34-minute elapsed interval. A proposed clearer rendering is “reduced using a
daily motion of 34′.” No numeral or source edition has been changed. See the
[published edition and original scans](sources.md), XII p.298.

### Repeated endpoint header: XII p.293

In the section headed October 3, the edition repeats an Aries-distance header
above the 8h0⅔m row. The printed page continues the Vulture sequence across the
column break and returns to Aries only at the subsequent *Rursus* label. The
catalog therefore identifies the 51°40′ separation as Mars–Vulture, preserving
the numeral and documenting the misleading added header separately. The same
heading's footnote reports October 1 in the manuscript; that is a source date
variant, without an adopted correction to the heading. See the
[published editions and scans](sources.md).

### XII p.45: lost Spica-column association

The simplified three-column transit tables leave two values without their original column label. Native scan geometry places both in the continuing **Spica west** column:

| Source locus | Value | Recovered quantity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 20 December 1590, Mars transit row | 15°30′ | Spica's west meridian distance |
| 28 December 1590, Mars transit row | 20°6′ | Spica's west meridian distance |

This is a confirmed loss of layout information, not a numerical transcription correction. Add the Spica-west label while retaining the separate Mars transit clock and the original numeral. The research catalog records the recovered quantity through scan-bound review; the original numeral remains preserved. The two affected loci retain separate citations. See the [public source registry](../src/data/public_sources.json) for volume XII.

The same page's longitude arithmetic differs by five arcseconds under exact subtraction of the stated correction. The printed source and final edition agree on those numbers. That arithmetic difference is retained as a historical audit result, without attributing an edition error or inferring a rounding procedure.

### Orion direction header: XII p.221

In the 18 February 1592 sequence, the repeated Orion column header says east even though the printed observing sequence has already switched to west before the later Aldebaran table. The catalog retains the angles 3°18′ and 4°25′ and identifies their westward direction from that continuation. The proposed correction concerns the repeated header, not the numbers. See the [published sources and original scan](sources.md).

The same scan confirms the printed Aldebaran distances of 24°46¾′ on 18 February and 33°24′ in the later sequence on 20 February. Their astronomical interpretation requires further analysis; these figures are preserved without a numerical transcription correction.

### Suspected altitude fraction: XII p.269

In the solar observations of 24 August 1593, explicitly collected for the Sun and
Mars, the edition gives the second mural-quadrant altitude as **41°40⅛′**. The
available scan appears to show **41°40⅓′**, but its small denominator has not been
settled confidently. This is a suspected transcription discrepancy requiring a
sharper independent witness. The catalog retains the edition’s one-eighth
fraction with a review flag; no numerical correction has been adopted. These are
solar altitudes, not Mars coordinates. See the [published sources](sources.md).

### Codex-qualified figures: XII p.279

Mars’s stated right ascension, 346°44⅓′, minus the stated ascension difference of
57°49′40″ gives 288°54′40″. The source instead prints Jupiter’s result as
288°54′20″, marked “thus in the codex.” The edition matches that printed value.
The catalog preserves the 20-arcsecond subtraction discrepancy as a historical
arithmetic check; no transcription correction is adopted. The same codex note
also qualifies the alternative Jupiter longitude of 17°21′15″ in Capricorn.
Its historical calculation procedure remains uncomputed. See the
[published editions and scans](sources.md).

### Morning and evening labels: XII p.285

The August 18 heading says morning, while the qualitative Mars–Jupiter diameter
comparison says “this evening.” Both labels agree with the original print.
Their relationship and the relevant day-count convention remain unresolved;
the catalog preserves both source claims without proposing an edition
correction, assigning an absolute observing time or inventing an angular size.

### Suspected declination terminology: XII p.286

The August 21 morning calculation renders **Declin. ♂ Merid.** as “Meridian
declination of Mars.” The print confirms the abbreviation. The following
calculation on p.287 renders the same qualifier “Southern”; another Mars passage
also renders *Decl. Merid.* as “southern declination,” making “South
declination of Mars” a candidate reading here. The terminology remains under
review: the catalog preserves 12°9½′ as an unsigned magnitude and does not
alter the edition or assign a hemisphere from that hypothesis. The following
comparison-star declination is explicitly south and retains that separate label.

### Missing clock denominator: XII p.288

The August 23 clock expression leaves the denominator unresolved in
10h47 2/[?]m. The printed page legibly gives **10h47²⁄₈m**. The proposed reading
preserves the literal two-eighths fraction; the canonical record remains
incomplete with a null numerical time until a correction is explicitly adopted.
See the [published editions and scans](sources.md).

### Repeated folio: Origanus's true-rising and setting table

The last leaf of the table beginning on p.241 is recorded with the printed
folio 256, following folio 257. The same leaf opens chapter IX and is native
source leaf 372. Its source locator retains this repeated folio separately from
the earlier folio 256. The usual folio offset is not applied to this exception.
This is a pagination finding, not a correction to an astronomical quantity.

### Clock pair and stated rule: XII pp.288–289

The August 24 middle clock pair is printed as 10h49½m to 10h40m corrected, a
9½-minute difference. The source later states an 8½-minute clock-fast rule,
which agrees with the first pair and the transit correction. The edition matches
the printed numerals and brace. The catalog retains the one-minute arithmetic
discrepancy without proposing an edition correction or changing recorded clocks.

### Printed motion comparison: XII pp.283–284

The source reports 4¾′ of direct motion from July 22 to July 25. Its printed
corresponding longitudes, 17°45′45″ and 17°51′20″ in Pisces, differ by 5′35″.
The final edition agrees with the original print at all three loci. The catalog
retains the 50″ arithmetic discrepancy without proposing an edition correction
or identifying the author's intended procedure. See the
[published editions and scans](sources.md).

### Displaced Mars altitude: XII p.294

In the October 30 sequence, the final edition puts **26°41′** in the Vulture-west
column and interleaves “altitude of Mars” and “by the steel instrument” with the
declination pair. The native page's side annotation identifies the figure as
Mars's altitude measured with that instrument, aligned with the 7h40m30s row.
The catalog preserves the number as an altitude and keeps the declinations
separate, without assigning them the steel instrument. This is a layout and
association problem; no numeral is changed. See the
[published editions and scans](sources.md).

### Proportional instrument table: X pp.19–20

The sixty argument/correction pairs are imported unchanged from the final
edition. Its stated anchor is 1°50′ at 48°. A direct proportional check makes
arguments 1–50 exact or half an arcsecond below that rule; arguments 51–60 are
10 or 10½ arcseconds below it. Equivalently, the last ten entries are 10″ below
integer truncation of the stated proportion.

This is an arithmetic consistency finding. Its origin remains unassigned
pending comparison of the table and anchor with the higher-resolution witness;
a numerical error in the final edition has not been established. Both the
recorded amounts and the diagnostic results are retained separately. See the
[public edition](https://arsastronomica.com/works/tycho-brahe-thesaurus-observationum-tomus-primus/).

### Repeated calibration header: XII p.445

The October 12 row containing Aldebaran's 16°20′ altitude has a 46°42′ angular
indication in the last column. The English table repeats a mouth-of-Pegasus-west
header there, but the native continuous column remains labeled Vulture west.
The catalog retains the number with the native reference and records the
header discrepancy separately. No numerical correction is made. The final
9h44½m clock and 15°40½′ stellar declination agree with the sharp printed witness.
See the [published editions and scans](sources.md).
